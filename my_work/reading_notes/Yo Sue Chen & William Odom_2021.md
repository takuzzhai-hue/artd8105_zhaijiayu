Crafting Temporality in Design

Amy Yo Sue Chen & William Odom (Simon Fraser University, Slow Technology Design Studio). In The Routledge International Handbook of Practice-based Research (2021), Chapter 3.5, pp. 368–380. DOI: 10.4324/9780429324154-27

## Why · Why Read This Chapter?

This chapter directly addresses the central direction of your revised research trajectory: the historical transformation of structures of delay and the concept of the “latent image / latency.”

Its central design project, Chronoscope—a tabletop digital photo viewer shaped like a telescope—essentially materializes temporality within a physical artifact. It encourages people to revisit their personal photographic archives slowly, deliberately, and non-chronologically.

This closely corresponds to your interests in delay, latent space, and the evocation of forgotten memories. The chapter even explicitly draws on Tulving’s concept of ecphory, or memory evocation.

More importantly, it demonstrates a practice-based research methodology centered on the designer-researcher. This provides a directly applicable methodological framework for transforming the act of “making something” into a form of academic research.

In terms of its citation network, the chapter connects Hallnäs and Redström’s concept of slow technology (2001), Frayling and Zimmerman’s work on Research through Design (RtD), and Schön’s concept of a reflective conversation with materials. It can therefore serve as a pivotal methodological reference for your research.

## D · Description / Summary

### Research Problem

Digital technologies enable the production of photographs at an estimated rate of 21,000 images per second, or 657 billion images annually. Personal digital archives have consequently become oversaturated, fragmented, and increasingly difficult to manage, making it challenging for them to support long-term meaningful activities such as remembering, reflection, and social connection.

Meanwhile, interaction design research methodologies struggle to keep pace with technological change.

The authors ask: How can we design digital artifacts that evolve over time and remain meaningful companions in everyday life? And how should such design research be conducted?

### The Project: Chronoscope

Chronoscope is a telescope-like monocular desktop device (Figs. 3.5.1–3.5.3).

It uses the timestamp metadata embedded in digital photograph files to reorganize an entire photographic archive along a temporal structure.

Its interaction is rotational and embodied:

- Rotating the black silicone surface allows users to move forward or backward through time: clockwise moves toward the future, while counterclockwise moves toward the past.
- Looking through the cyan eyepiece reveals a magnified individual photograph.
- A side knob adjusts the timeline mode (linear, date, or time) and the granularity of navigation, allowing each rotational increment to move through between 1 and 300 photographs.

The telescope-like form deliberately suggests focused, exploratory viewing.

### Core Contribution: The Designer-Researcher Approach

Using Chronoscope as its central case study, the chapter proposes a practice-based research approach in which highly finished design artifacts constitute research in their own right.

The approach is organized around four pillars:

1\. Research through Design (RtD)

Physical design artifacts are used to investigate wicked problems. Research outcomes are examined through four evaluative lenses:

- Process: Whether the process is reproducible and supported by a clear rationale.
- Invention: Whether the design builds upon concepts capable of addressing the research question.
- Relevance: Whether the work moves existing conditions toward a more desirable state.
- Extensibility: Whether its insights can inform future research and design practices.

2\. Autobiographical Design (AD)

Designers treat their own everyday lives as sites of investigation.

The authors further identify four specific priorities within their approach: originality, diversity, provocativeness, and unity.

Diversity involves small, interdisciplinary design teams, while unity emphasizes a coherent narrative voice.

3\. Reflective Conversation with Materials (Schön)

In this project, the primary “material” consists of the timestamp metadata associated with digital photographs.

Through repeated prototyping and reflective engagement with this material, the design gradually evolved from a linear timeline toward non-chronological timeframe modes.

4\. Research Product

The design artifact is refined into a finished product capable of being lived with over an extended period in real domestic environments.

This involves two stages: producing a durable and sufficiently finished artifact, and subsequently living with it over time.

### How the Method Generates Design Decisions

This is one of the chapter’s most compelling methodological insights.

The design team lived with the finished product themselves, encountered experiential frictions, and then returned to revise the design.

For example, with an archive containing 20,000 photographs, navigating one image per rotational increment would require approximately 2.77 hours to reach a particular photograph.

This produced an embodied sensation of being “stuck in time.”

The designers consequently introduced adjustable navigation granularity, ranging from 1 to 300 photographs per rotation.

However, when granularity became too high, users could experience the opposite sensation: being “lost in time.”

The team therefore established upper and lower limits for granularity.

Slowness is not merely a romantic design ideal. It becomes a design parameter repeatedly calibrated through embodied friction.

### Conclusion

Embedding slowness and temporality within an artifact requires additional time for reflection and adaptation.

This directly conflicts with contemporary interaction design conventions that prioritize speed and efficiency. The authors therefore argue that design methodology itself must evolve.

They invite collaboration between philosophers, social scientists, and practitioners experienced in manipulating temporal media, including musicians, composers, poets, and new media artists, to translate theoretical concepts of time into creative practice.

## V · Critical Reflection

### 1. Almost No External Evaluation

The chapter is predominantly an autobiographical, first-person reflection by the design team.

Although it mentions deploying the artifact in people’s homes, this chapter does not report the results of a user study. Instead, those findings are addressed in the original DIS 2019 paper.

This is acceptable for a methodological book chapter. However, the central claim that a research product should be lived with over time is supported here primarily by the design team’s own experiences, rather than longitudinal data from external participants.

This creates a clear risk of self-validation.

### 2. Sample Size: The Design Team Itself

This reflects a recurring problem in autobiographical design and designer-researcher methodologies: objectivity and generalizability.

Insights such as being “stuck in time” or “lost in time” emerge from the embodied experiences of a very small group of designers.

Whether these experiences also apply to ordinary users remains unverified.

Although the authors acknowledge difficulties in objectively reporting autobiographical design research, they do not fully resolve them.

### 3. The Assumption That “Slow” Is Better Remains Unexamined

Throughout the chapter, slowness is implicitly associated with reflection and meaningful experience, while speed and information overload are treated as problems.

However, slow technology may itself presuppose a position of privilege or leisure.

Who has the time to be slow?

This stands in contrast to Albers’s aesthetics of abundance, which approaches quantity as an aesthetic object rather than lamenting information overload.

It also resonates with Cross’s warning that preserving everyday experience may dilute critical engagement.

The chapter lacks this level of self-criticism.

### 4. The Narrowing of Timestamp Metadata into the Sole “Material”

The project primarily engages with timestamps, reducing the richness of photographic experience to sortable numerical metadata.

This is an intelligent engineering decision, but it also risks equating the temporality of memory with the temporality of a file system.

This offers a productive point of critique from media archaeology and phenomenology:

The temporality of memory is not equivalent to the GMT timestamp of a digital file.

### 5. Conceptual Overlap Among the Four Methodological Pillars

RtD, autobiographical design, reflective conversation with materials, and research product overlap considerably.

All four emphasize first-person experience, iteration, and engagement with materials.

The chapter does not clearly distinguish their conceptual boundaries.

As a result, the framework sometimes reads as a consolidation of several methodologies previously developed by Odom’s research team, rather than an entirely new methodological model.

Its most distinctive addition may be the emphasis on unity as a coherent narrative voice.

## R · Creative Reflection

### 1. A Direct Reference for Your Latent Image / Latency Research

The two experiential extremes described through Chronoscope—being “stuck in time” and being “lost in time”—could be reinterpreted as two pathological conditions of delay:

- Zero latency: Immediate appearance, with no period of latency.
- Infinite latency: An image that never fully develops or becomes visible.

Your concept of the latent image offers a third possibility between these extremes: a structural form of waiting in which something has not yet appeared but remains capable of appearing in the future.

The chapter provides a useful engineering-oriented reference for this argument.

The designers use upper and lower limits of granularity to regulate temporal experience into a rhythm that can be lived with.

In contrast, you could argue that latency should not necessarily be regulated away or eliminated; it should be preserved as a constitutive structure of the artwork.

### 2. Transferring “Reflective Conversation with Materials” to Your Practice

Schön’s framework can be extended to your photographic and sound-based practice.

Your materials are not limited to metadata. They could include:

- The development time of latent photographic images
- Afterglow and latency in elongated LED displays
- Reverberation tails in sound

These material phenomena could serve as active participants in the research process rather than merely technical properties to be controlled.

### 3. A Different Method for Addressing a Related Problem

Chen and Odom use Research through Design and slow technology to address digital overload and long-term relationships with digital artifacts.

You could approach a related set of questions through media archaeology and embodied phenomenology.

Rather than asking:

How can we design a slow artifact that allows people to revisit their photographs more meaningfully?

You could ask:

How has latency itself, as a condition of mediation, been repeatedly produced, transformed, and eliminated by different imaging technologies throughout history?

For example:

- The waiting involved in silver-halide photographic development
- The apparent immediacy of digital photography
- The computational waiting introduced by sampling processes in generative latent spaces

This question captures the central concern of your proposed historical study of the transformation of latency structures.

### 4. Ecphory as a Valuable Conceptual Resource

The chapter draws on Tulving’s concept of ecphory, in which sensory input triggers the retrieval or reactivation of vague or forgotten memories, to explain the non-chronological timeframe mode.

This concept could become particularly useful in your dissertation.

You could develop an analogy between the development of a latent photographic image and ecphory in memory:

A latent trace is activated after a period of delay and subsequently emerges into awareness.

This establishes a potential cognitive-scientific reference for a threefold conceptual relationship:

Latent image / Latent space / Latent memory

### 5. Methodological Relevance to Your Practice-Based Research

This chapter demonstrates how making a product or prototype can become publishable practice-based research.

Chronoscope, with its attempt to embed temporality into photographic interaction, also provides an existing academic reference for your investigations into memory sovereignty.

In particular, your ideas about “helping users let go” and “dual-layer curation” could be examined in relation to its adjustable granularity and timeframe modes.

Excluding the references, the main text occupies approximately ten pages (pp. 368–377).

It follows the conventions of a handbook chapter.

Structural principle: The chapter establishes a methodological framework consisting of four pillars, discusses each pillar in turn, and continually returns to the same design project as its central example.

### Where the Contribution Is First Introduced

The contribution is initially framed through a series of research questions at the end of the introduction, including:

“How can we advance design processes…” and “How can technology be designed to become longer-term everyday resources…” (p. 368).

The methodological contribution is formally named at the beginning of “The Designer-Researcher Approach” (p. 371).

The primary contribution is methodological rather than empirical.

This is characteristic of a methodology-oriented handbook chapter.

### Key Contribution Statement

> “Over the past several years, we have adopted what we call a designer-researcher approach to making and reflecting on highly finished design artifacts as a form of practice-based research in and of itself.” (p. 371)

### Description Versus Analysis

The chapter is weighted toward analysis.

The description of Chronoscope is concentrated in the first two pages, while the remainder uses the project to reflect on methodological questions.

The artifact serves as evidence; the methodology constitutes the argument.

### How Related Work Is Used

The chapter constructs an intellectual lineage to position its contribution:

Slow technology → Research through Design → Autobiographical Design → Reflective Conversation → Research Product

It also refers to several earlier projects by the same research group, including Photobox, Olly, Olo Radio, CrescendoMessage, and FamilyStories, demonstrating the continuity of its research program.

This is more an approach of building an intellectual lineage than identifying a narrow research gap and arguing against existing positions.

### How the Design Process Becomes Evidence

The chapter relies on first-person process narratives, concrete numerical examples, and three figures.

Its central evidence includes specific friction points, such as:

- An archive of 20,000 photographs
- Approximately 2.77 hours required to navigate through the archive
- Approximately 0.5 seconds per rotation
- The embodied experiences of being “stuck in time” and “lost in time”

These details make the design process concrete and communicable.

There are no statistical analyses or direct quotations from external users.

### Figures

The chapter contains three figures, all primarily explanatory.

Figure 3.5.1: Shows the artifact in a domestic environment, emphasizing its presence as a long-term everyday companion.

Figure 3.5.2: Uses a three-part visual explanation to demonstrate embodied interaction: rotating the surface, looking through the eyepiece, and adjusting the side knob.

Figure 3.5.3: Shows the interface within the eyepiece, with a central photograph surrounded by temporal positioning information.

Together, the figures explain the artifact’s appearance and interaction mechanisms.

### Narrative Voice and Tense

The chapter predominantly uses the first-person plural “we” and the past tense, with expressions such as “we designed,” “we settled,” and “we arrived at.”

This establishes the text as a retrospective account of a completed design process.

Unlike artistic research papers that may rely on the singular “I,” this chapter adopts the collective voice of a research team.

That choice also reinforces its methodological emphasis on unity as a coherent narrative voice.

### Limitations

There is no dedicated limitations section.

However, the chapter includes multiple accounts of design frictions, which function as acknowledged difficulties within the research process.

A distinctive feature of the chapter is that it reframes limitations as methodological resources.

Friction becomes a source of design insight rather than simply a weakness.

This is transparent in one sense, but it also allows the chapter to avoid a direct evaluation of whether the resulting artifact actually works effectively for users.

## Venue · Publication Context and Expectations

The chapter appears in Routledge’s The Routledge International Handbook of Practice-based Research, rather than as a conference or journal paper.

This type of publication particularly values teachable and transferable methodological frameworks.

It rewards the ability to transform a concrete design project into a research approach that others can adopt, while also encouraging interdisciplinary dialogue.

The conclusion’s invitation to philosophers, social scientists, and artists reflects this orientation.

A handbook chapter does not necessarily require extensive empirical validation or direct confrontation with competing theoretical positions.

Its primary purpose is to communicate a research approach and explain how it can be practiced.

The authors discuss empirical aspects of their work elsewhere, including in DIS 2019 and related CHI publications. This chapter instead concentrates on distilling the methodological contribution.

For your research, it offers a strong model for writing a chapter on practice as research: how to transform the development of a single artifact into an articulated and transferable methodology.

However, its genre differs substantially from journals such as Photography and Culture, Visual Studies, and Screen.

Those journals generally place greater emphasis on critical theory, conceptual argumentation, and close analysis of visual or media texts.

The Routledge handbook prioritizes methodological clarity and practical transferability.

You can borrow its methodological structure, but the argumentative approach should be adapted to your humanities-oriented, phenomenological research trajectory.
