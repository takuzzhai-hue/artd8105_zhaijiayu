

Eye of the Beholder / Eye II (1968) - Suzanne Hanauer, Manfred Schroeder

1. Who they are + the work itself
Manfred R. Schroeder (1926–2009): A German physicist who joined Bell Labs in 1954 and became director of acoustics and speech research at Murray Hill in 1963. He is best known for his work in acoustics (the Schroeder reverberator, the Schroeder frequency) and speech coding (the linear predictive coding developed with Bishnu Atal, which underlies today's cellular voice compression). His image-making was, in fact, a by-product of his research into the visualization of speech data.
Suzanne Hanauer: A programmer at Bell Labs in the 1960s and a long-uncredited collaborator—the code she wrote was the very precondition for these images to exist.
The work: Eye of the Beholder / Eye II (1968) fills the left eye of a female portrait with the characters of the proverb "ONE PICTURE IS WORTH A THOUSAND WORDS," placed cell by cell across a grid. Up close it reads as blurry, typewriter-like text; from a distance it resolves into a single enormous human eye. The piece thus becomes a pun on the proverb: this "picture" is literally made of a thousand words.
2. The moment they were working in
Technology: Bell Labs' IBM 7094 paired with the Stromberg-Carlson SC 4020 microfilm plotter (installed in 1961). The machine could illuminate over a million points on a CRT, but it could not produce gray directly—so "gray" had to be faked through the number of exposures or the density of symbols. This limitation is the technical root of the work.
Art / theory: 1968 was a breakout year for computer art—London's Cybernetic Serendipity, Zagreb's New Tendencies movement and its bit international journal, and the Bense–Moles "information aesthetics" out of Stuttgart. Schroeder's paper was reprinted in bit No. 2 alongside that of his colleague A. Michael Noll, together serving as an endorsement, from inside the laboratory itself, of the claim that computer-generated images could be art.
Contemporaries: A. Michael Noll; Ken Knowlton & Leon Harmon (also at Bell Labs, making the symbol-halftone reclining nude); Frieder Nake; Georg Nees; Vera Molnár.
Influence: It established the paradigm of simulating grayscale through character density / exposure—the direct ancestor of ASCII art, digital halftoning, and the mosaic portrait.
3. The rule / procedure (precise enough for a stranger to execute)
1. Take a continuous-tone black-and-white portrait photograph, focusing on the eye region.
2. Lay a grid of 128 × 128 = 16,384 points over that region.
3. Take the text "ONE PICTURE IS WORTH A THOUSAND WORDS," repeat it in a loop, and assign one character (a letter or a blank space) to each grid point in sequence.
4. Measure the brightness value of the original photograph at each grid point.
5. Map brightness to number of exposures: when photographing the screen, the SC 4020 flashes that point's character 1 to 40 times—more flashes → darker, fewer flashes → lighter (blank-space points barely flash and become highlights).
6. Output to microfilm and enlarge. → Up close, repeating blurry text; from a distance, the human eye's low-pass integration fuses it into an eye.
One-sentence version: "Slice a photo of an eye into a 128×128 grid, drop the next character of 'one picture is worth a thousand words' into each cell, then flash it as many times as that cell needs to be dark (1 to 40)—step back, and the words become an eye again."
4. Why this connects to me
Phenomenology of the image / embodiment: The image does not reside on the paper but is generated in the act of viewing across distance—up close it is linguistic symbols; only from afar does it "appear" as a bodily organ (the eye). And what it depicts is an eye—the organ of seeing is itself seen, so "the beholder's eye" becomes literal, striking straight at the themes of gaze and perception.
Media archaeology: How a technical illustration buried in an engineering paper comes to be recognized as an "artwork"—how it is named, and how it is re-attributed—makes this a perfect case study of the identity of the computer image; and "exposures = grayscale" is itself a pivotal moment in the history of the digital image.
Through exposure and digital computation, characters are printed onto the paper, and the very core of the image becomes a point-by-point, computable restatement; optical recording is swapped for the SC 4020's mechanical gaze, flashing 1 to 40 times, cell by cell.
