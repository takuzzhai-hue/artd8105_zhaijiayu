Reading Notes: “I'm happy even though it's not real”: GenAI Photo Editing as a Remembering Experience
Yufeng Wu, Qing Li, Elise van den Hoven & A. Baki Kocaballi (University of Technology Sydney), CHI 2026, 18 pages. DOI: 10.1145/3772318.3790927.

1 · Why I Read This Paper
This paper reconceptualises generative AI photo editing as an act of remembering rather than merely a process of image production. Unlike conventional studies that focus on the authenticity of AI-generated images and the risks of false memories, it investigates how users negotiate discrepancies between visual records and subjective memories when actively editing their personal photographs.

This establishes a direct academic dialogue with my project, Re-developing Memory, in which photographic images are reprocessed according to written descriptions of personal memories.
2 · Summary
- What the paper is about: Through photo-editing tasks and semi-structured interviews with 12 participants, the study investigates how generative AI participates in personal remembering.
- Main contributions: Drawing on the concept of Remembering Experience (RX), the authors examine psychological states, social contexts, physical contexts, and memory content during photo editing, proposing design implications for responsible AI-assisted photo editing.
- What problem it addresses and why it matters: Previous research has predominantly focused on deception and memory distortion caused by AI-manipulated photographs. However, actively modifying photographs may also constitute a form of personal expression and a means of reinterpreting the past.
- Innovation and benefits: The study positions photo editing as part of the remembering process itself, rather than an image-making activity that occurs after remembering.
- What it advances: The findings suggest that participants sometimes prioritise subjective feelings over factual accuracy. They also demonstrate different boundaries of acceptance regarding modifications to environmental elements and modifications involving personal identity.
3 · Critical Thinking
- Subjective authenticity does not necessarily equal memory authenticity. Participants' perceptions that edited photographs better represent their feelings do not establish that these photographs are closer to their original experiences. Instead, the edited images may reflect their present emotional states and desires.
- A lack of longitudinal validation. I am particularly interested in whether participants would still be able to distinguish their original memories from subsequently generated visual content when revisiting the edited photographs several months later.
- Insufficient discussion of photography as a medium. The paper primarily examines the relationship between users and AI-assisted editing behaviours rather than investigating why photographic images can carry memories. Photographic indexicality, optical imaging, and technological mediation remain underexplored.
- The distinction between editing agency and memory sovereignty. The ability to enter prompts does not necessarily mean that users have complete control over the reconstruction of their memories. The model's interpretation of language, default aesthetic preferences, and generative mechanisms may introduce new power relations.
- Limited generalisability. A qualitative study involving 12 participants is appropriate for identifying patterns of experience, but its findings cannot establish universal principles of human memory.
4 · Creative Thinking
- New ideas inspired by the paper: A photograph need not function solely as a record of past experience. It can also become an operational interface through which past experiences are reorganised.
- Methodological transfer: I could incorporate comparative interviews involving the original photograph, intermediate images, and final output into my own system, investigating at which stage participants experience the strongest sense of remembering.
- Alternative approaches to the same problem: While this paper approaches memory reconstruction through modifications to photographic content, my research could approach it through photographic imaging forms. Rather than directly altering objects within the image, I could manipulate the visibility of memory through medium-specific properties such as focus, exposure, noise, colour, and occlusion.
- New research directions: Participants' acceptance, rejection, and reversal of individual image transformations could be documented as process-based evidence of memory sovereignty. The final artwork would no longer constitute the sole research outcome; the participants' decision-making processes could also become research materials.
5 · Form (Reading the Paper as a Genre)
- Overall length: 18 pages, including references.
- Structural characteristics: The argument is primarily organised around the Introduction, Related Work, Research Methods, Findings, and Discussion. Compared with a SIGGRAPH demonstration paper, it more closely follows the structure of a conventional empirical HCI paper.
- First appearance of the contribution claim: The abstract establishes the research subject, methodology, and principal findings. The Introduction subsequently develops the research gap.
- System description vs. analysis: The system itself is not the sole contribution. Participants' interpretations of their photo-editing experiences constitute the central analytical focus.
- Approach to related work: The research context is established through literature on remembering, the relationship between photography and memory, generative AI editing, and its associated risks.
- How the process is evidenced: The argument is supported through participants' edited photographs, interview materials, and the researchers' interpretation and categorisation of their experiences.
- Figures: Fig. 1 connects original photographs, different edited versions, and participants' experiences, explaining both the research subject and the experimental logic.
- Person and tense: Research activities are described using the first-person plural and past tense, while theoretical discussions and research implications predominantly employ the present tense.
- Limitations: Particular attention should be paid to the boundaries of the evidence concerning sample size, contexts of use, and the long-term effects of AI editing on memory.
6 · Venue Signal
This CHI paper demonstrates how an interactive system related to artistic practice can establish an HCI contribution through clearly defined research questions, user studies, and design implications.

Unlike Flashover, it does not primarily establish its research value through the visual qualities of an artwork. Instead, its argument is grounded in participants' behaviours and interpretations.

For my own research, submitting a photographic interaction system to CHI would require more than an explanation of its artistic concept and aesthetic qualities. It would also require an investigation of what users actually experience through interacting with the system.
7 · Take-home
- What I learned: Image editing can constitute an act of remembering rather than merely an outcome of remembering.
- What was new to me: Subjective feelings and factual accuracy do not always coincide. Users also establish different boundaries of acceptance when modifying different photographic elements.
- What I can apply to my research: Process-based interviews and comparisons between image versions could transform intermediate photographic states into analysable research materials.
- Whether the structure is worth emulating: Yes. It is particularly valuable as a structural reference for developing photographic practice into an HCI paper supported by user experience research.
