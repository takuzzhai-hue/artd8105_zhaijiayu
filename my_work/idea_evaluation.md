## 1 · Introduction

**Working title: The Continuous Body That Delay Takes Away**

**General background.**

A photograph has no explicit time axis, yet every photographed event takes place over a duration. Photographic exposure integrates light over time, while video and computational imaging allow discrete moments to be sampled, selected, and recomposed after capture. Both operations can make duration visible in a still image, but they preserve different aspects of an event. This project approaches *delay* not simply as waiting or technical latency, but as the location and process through which time becomes an image.

**General motivation for your work.**

I want to make the transformation of continuous movement into a still photograph visible and tangible. Rather than explaining the difference between exposure and computation only through theory, I will use the same few seconds of moving bodies, falling rain, and wind-blown leaves to make two prints. Their juxtaposition will invite viewers to notice not only what each photograph shows, but also what each operation removes.

**The specific problem you want to address.**

When the same brief event is made into a still photograph through temporal integration or temporal sampling, which aspects of its movement remain visible and which disappear? How does moving the decisive temporal operation from exposure to post-capture computation change what can be chosen, revised, and perceived? I call the missing continuity of an identifiable moving body **the continuous body that delay takes away**.

## 2 · New problem or old problem?

**An old problem, approached through a new comparison and a proposed intermediate-level concept.**

Photography has long represented duration through long exposure and chronophotography, and contemporary artists also combine distinct instants into a single picture. Étienne-Jules Marey developed techniques to analyse successive phases of movement; Hiroshi Sugimoto's extended exposures show one powerful form of temporal accumulation; Pelle Cass's time-composite photographs gather separate moments into one frame. These are important precedents rather than problems they failed to solve. Their work means that neither the accumulation of motion nor the repetition of bodies is, by itself, new.

What remains worth investigating in *this project* is what becomes legible when **both temporal operations are applied to one source event and exhibited as a pair**, with delay's location serving as the comparison. An integration-oriented print privileges a continuous trace but weakens the readable body and sequence; a sampling-oriented print preserves separated bodies while omitting the movement between selected instants. The paired arrangement allows the viewer to compare these distinct absences directly.

The proposed contribution is therefore **not** a newly invented photographic technique or a claim that all still photography has only two ways of handling time. It is a specific conceptual framing—*the continuous body that delay takes away*—developed through a materially controlled pair of photographs. Whether the framing extends beyond this instance remains a question for the paper, not a finding already established.

## 3 · Methods

**What are your methods? List their major features.**

1. **Capture one short event.** Record a fixed-view, wide/panoramic video lasting a few seconds. Include a stable background and independently moving elements such as people, rain, and leaves. Keep the same framing, duration, and source footage for the two treatments.
2. **A — temporal integration.** Accumulate motion information across the recorded duration to form a continuous-looking white trace over the stationary scene. White is an intentional graphic encoding of movement without a legible body, **not** a claim that physical long exposure automatically produces white lines. Document whether the accumulation uses additive light values, extracted motion paths, or a hybrid procedure.
3. **B — temporal sampling.** Choose frames at a stated interval **Δt**, segment the moving subjects, and place their silhouettes or bodies back at their corresponding image positions in one still. Keep the interval and compositing rules explicit; where overlaps occur, document the chosen layering order rather than treating it as a natural record of chronology.
4. **Compare and exhibit.** Print A and B at the same size and display them side by side. Analyse visible continuity, body legibility, temporal order, and omitted intervals. Produce a small **Δt** variation series to show how changing the computational sampling interval changes B.
5. **Check the technical distinction.** Where feasible, capture a simultaneous physical long exposure for comparison with video-derived A. Since video frames already integrate light over short shutter intervals, describe A and B as *different weightings and arrangements of exposure-integrated samples*, not as perfectly pure opposing mechanisms.

**What are their novelties and advantages?**

The distinctive design decision is the **controlled pairing of two stills made from the same short event**, with the location of delay as the interpretive variable. The pair holds subject and viewpoint largely constant so that viewers can attribute differences to how duration is folded into the image. Using prints rather than playback also forces both strategies to confront the limits of a static surface. The adjustable **Δt** series gives a visible way to interrogate the decisions made after capture.

**Can your methods deliver results that could not be achieved before? If not, what are the improvements?**

No unprecedented image-making capability is claimed: long exposure and multi-moment composites have established histories. The expected improvement is **conceptual and comparative**, not technical. Making these two treatments from one video could give a precise visual demonstration of two different kinds of temporal omission and a concrete object around which to discuss the transfer of selection from capture to post-capture computation. The work will need to show whether viewers can actually distinguish these effects; the prints alone cannot prove universal claims about perception or photographic history.

## 4 · Contribution type — declared

**Type:** Intermediate-level concept.

**Why this type:**

The principal outcome is neither a novel compositing algorithm nor an empirical generalisation about viewers. It is a concept arising from a particular designed artifact: **the continuous body that delay takes away**. In A, continuity appears as a trace without a fully readable body or direction; in B, identifiable bodies remain, while the intervals connecting them are omitted. The concept names the tension between these two absences and directs attention to **where the image's temporal decisions take place**—during exposure or after capture.

The concept is deliberately narrower than a theory of all photography. It is grounded in the specific pair, and it may offer a way to discuss other interfaces that turn continuous activity into fixed, selectively revisable representations (for example, time-lapse composites or movement summaries). Those possible transfers must be argued and tested through comparison, not assumed.

**What evidence this type requires:**

- The finished paired prints and full documentation of the shared input footage, framing, exposure, processing, and compositing decisions.
- A close visual annotation identifying where each image preserves continuity, body, positional sequence, and where information becomes illegible or absent.
- A small **Δt** variation study and, if feasible, a real-long-exposure comparison that expose the limits of the integration/sampling distinction.
- A careful positioning against Marey, Sugimoto, and Pelle Cass, plus at least one justified example of a *different* design situation the concept might illuminate. The comparison should articulate the limits of transfer as well as the similarities.

## 5 · Contributions

> The major contributions of this paper are:
> 1. **A controlled comparison of two temporal losses.** Existing duration-based photographic techniques are often encountered as separate aesthetic strategies, making their different omissions difficult to isolate. By transforming **the same brief footage** into an integration-oriented trace and a sampling-oriented arrangement of bodies, the project makes continuity, bodily legibility, and omitted intervals directly comparable. **Benefit:** a concrete visual basis for discussing what a still image can and cannot carry from a moving event.
> 2. **An intermediate-level concept: “the continuous body that delay takes away.”** Discussions of photographic time can remain at the level of exposure technology or image appearance without identifying the distinct absence made by each operation. By annotating the paired prints around the missing continuous moving body, the project proposes a transferable critical vocabulary for analysing still representations of dynamic events. **Benefit:** a focused concept that connects material image-making to questions of temporal perception without claiming a universal theory.
> 3. **A situated account of where delay is decided.** Treating computation as simply a new visual effect obscures the difference between time integrated during capture and moments chosen after capture. Through a documented compositing process, an adjustable **Δt** study, and (if feasible) a physical-exposure comparison, the project examines how post-capture selection introduces reversibility and editorial control. **Benefit:** a more precise way to discuss how photographic agency changes when temporal decisions move from exposure into computation.

## 7 · The necessity test

Both must fail for the project to be coherent.

- **Remove the making — is the finding still supported?** **No, not in the form claimed.** Without the two photographs made from the *same* footage, “integration loses bodies / sampling loses intervals” risks remaining a familiar technical observation. The actual images are necessary to discover how traces, segmentation failures, overlap, transparency, occlusion, and print scale complicate that neat opposition. Making is where the proposed concept encounters evidence and possible counterexamples; it is not just an illustration added after the argument.
- **Remove the domain or site — is the method still a contribution?** **No, not as this contribution is framed.** The question depends on the photographic transformation of a duration into a bounded, fixed image and on the history of exposure, chronophotography, and computational compositing. Without this medium-specific tension, the pair becomes a generic data-visualisation exercise. A broader application may follow, but would require a new account of which parts of the concept survive outside photography.

**Result:** **Provisionally passes both necessity tests.** The artifact and photographic domain are essential to the intended intermediate-level concept. The strongest remaining risk is that the two losses appear to be obvious properties of integration and sampling rather than a fresh contribution. The final work must demonstrate something the comparison makes newly *noticeable*—especially the different locations of delay, the ambiguities of direction and continuity, and the viewer's attempt to mentally connect the absent movement—without asserting audience effects that have not been studied.
