## Work Concept

Does a photograph have only one final form?

When we press the shutter button, the camera typically transforms the optical information it receives into a photograph that can be viewed through a series of predefined image-processing procedures. Throughout this process, the image passes through various intermediate states: it may be blurred, devoid of colour, or filled with noise; it may also exhibit different tonal values, colour temperatures, and spatial relationships. Yet these unfinished images usually remain invisible to us. Through a predetermined imaging pipeline, they are eventually transformed into what is considered a correct and complete photograph.

However, I believe that these intermediate states, which are corrected during the imaging process, are equally possible forms of existence for the photograph. I wish to reopen this ordinarily invisible process, bringing the various possibilities of a photograph before it reaches its final form back into our field of vision.

The work begins with a personal photograph. Participants upload a photograph they have taken and write about their memories of the photographed scene, or their feelings upon revisiting the image in the present. Based on these textual descriptions, the system draws upon the camera's internal image-processing mechanisms, as well as the regeneration of pictorial space, to gradually reveal the different forms that the photograph could take.

For instance, when someone wishes to forget something within a photograph, it may gradually disappear from the image, leaving behind an empty space. When a memory becomes fragmented or confused, the image may return to an unfinished state, exhibiting noise, defocus, displacement, and visual deterioration. When the gaze within a memory is not directed towards the original subject of the photograph, the focal point may shift to another part of the image. Alternatively, the system may extend the original photographic space, reconstructing parts of the remembered scene that were never captured, or even allowing something that once existed but has since disappeared to reappear within the image.

Each transformation becomes a possible form of the photograph. These forms are gradually developed, juxtaposed, and layered, eventually giving rise to a new image of memory.

In Henri Bergson's philosophy, the past is not a collection of fixed images that are preserved intact and waiting to be retrieved. Rather, memory exists virtually. Each act of recollection brings a part of the past into the present, allowing it to acquire a concrete form. Consequently, the same memory may take on different forms when recalled at different moments. Sometimes, a particular detail suddenly becomes clear while other elements recede into shadow. Sometimes, we remember the presence of a person but can no longer recall their face. At other times, the space within our memory is far more expansive than the photograph has recorded.

Through this system, I seek to draw upon the ordinarily invisible processes within the camera to make the formation of the photographic image itself visible. In doing so, I hope to explore the poetry inherent in the photographic medium, allowing the photograph to become an image that can continually be revisited, reorganised, and developed anew.

## Work Process

Participants upload a photograph they have taken and write about their memories of the photographed scene or their reflections on the photograph itself.

Based on these textual descriptions, the system progressively generates a series of image transformations inspired by the possible states and effects within the camera's imaging pipeline. The resulting images are then assembled into a composite through the juxtaposition of different visual states, ultimately producing a new photographic artwork.
