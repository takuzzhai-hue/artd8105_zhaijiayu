# Topic Declaration

## Territory

My research focuses on rethinking the photographic act as photography shifts from classical optical and material processes toward computational and AI systems.

I want to approach this question from two directions.

The first looks backward to classical photographic processes. I am interested in taking mechanisms such as light, exposure, photosensitive materials, and development out of the conventional camera and reconstructing them as interactive and embodied installations. By making these processes physically experienceable, I want to reconsider what it means to “take a photograph” before photography becomes only an image.

The second looks forward to computational photography and learned models. When AI becomes part of the photographic process, pressing the shutter no longer necessarily determines the final image. Photographing can continue through reconstruction, generation, selection, and rejection. This changes both the photographic act and the role of the photographer.

These two directions approach the same question from opposite sides:

classical photographic processes ← photographic act → computational / AI systems

Through practice, I want to investigate how these changing technical conditions might also require us to rethink photographic concepts such as apparatus, index, and authorship.

## Where it came from

The theoretical background currently connects to my existing interests in photographic phenomenology, embodiment, media archaeology, and the relationship between photographic traces and computational images.

## The rung

Capture

I choose capture because my work still begins with the act of photographing something in the physical world. The encounter between the photographer, apparatus, and world remains important to me.

At the same time, I am particularly interested in the seam between:

capture ↔ learned models

I want to explore what happens when a learned model enters the process after a photograph has been taken.

This also changes where I locate authorship. The photographer may no longer specify every part of the final image. Instead, authorship can involve framing, curating, constraining, and vetoing what the computational system produces.

So I am choosing capture not simply because I want to use a camera, but because I want to investigate what the act of photographing becomes when capture is no longer the end of the photographic process.

## The field

My main field is contemporary photographic theory and practice, particularly where photography intersects with computational media, AI, and interactive art.

My practice also draws on media archaeology and embodiment. Looking backward at historical photographic apparatuses helps me examine the material and bodily conditions of photography, while working with learned models allows me to test how those conditions are changing now.

The theoretical contribution I want to develop from these experiments is a reconsideration of the photographic act after AI: how concepts such as the apparatus, index, and authorship might change when the camera becomes part of a larger computational system.

## Candidate venues

SIGGRAPH — my primary candidate, particularly for experimental work combining photography, interactive apparatuses, computation, and learned models.

ISEA — International Symposium on Electronic Art — for practice-based research connecting photographic processes, interactive installation, media archaeology, and computational art.

Photography and Culture — for the theoretical development of the research, particularly changing ideas of photography, indexicality, and photographic practice under computational conditions.

These venues are provisional and still need to be verified.

## What I would make

I want to develop a series of interactive photographic installations, with each work taking one specific photographic mechanism, phenomenon, or theoretical concept and turning it into an experience that can be physically encountered and tested.

Some works may begin with fundamental photographic phenomena, such as focus, exposure, light, or the relationship between the body and the apparatus, and transform them into interactive installations. Others may introduce computational and learned systems, allowing the viewer's body, movement, or memory to participate in the production and transformation of the image.

Each installation would function as a small practical experiment around a specific theoretical question. By moving between classical photographic mechanisms and contemporary computational systems, I want to use these works to test where existing photographic theories remain useful, where they begin to fail, and what new concepts might be needed for the future of photography.

The series therefore moves through:

photographic phenomenon → interactive experiment → theoretical question → possible new understanding of photography
