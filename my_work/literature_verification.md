## Raw report — unverified

Literature Review: Two Ways of Folding Time into a Still Photograph
Adversarial prior-art review · Photography, computational imaging, HCI, and visual communication design · October 2026

Preliminary verdict
Your project has an established technical foundation, a heavily precedented photographic form, and a narrower—but potentially defensible—research question.
The basic distinction between temporal integration and temporal sampling is not new. Neither is turning continuous motion into traces, extracting successive bodies from a recording, or collapsing a duration into one photograph.

More importantly, I found two computational-photography papers that challenge your proposed distinction between what happens during exposure and what happens after capture:

- Tabellion et al. (2023), Computational Long Exposure Mobile Photography. A sequence of short exposures can be computationally processed into a long-exposure appearance, with synthetic motion blur and preserved sharp subjects. The visible result does not reliably tell us where the temporal integration occurred.
- Rengarajan et al. (2019), Photosequencing of Motion Blur using Short and Long Exposures. A blurred exposure can be combined with short-exposure observations to recover a sequence of sharp images. This directly challenges any categorical claim that temporal integration permanently removes the order of movement.

These are not necessarily demonstrations that your two-print artwork already exists. They are stronger objections to the theory you currently use to explain it.

The central issue is therefore not whether a still photograph can hold time. That has been demonstrated repeatedly. It is whether your paired photographic translations can expose a meaningful difference in what they preserve, suppress, and make perceptible—without claiming that their technical mechanisms are mutually exclusive.

### 1. What is already established

A. Photographic history: your two visual outcomes are not new

- Étienne-Jules Marey — Chronophotography, 1882–1894. Marey exposed successive phases of movement onto one photographic plate using an intermittent shutter. He also used dark clothing and white or reflective markings to isolate bodily movement. The report says this is damaging to the originality of the white-line photograph, and that temporal sampling can occur inside the camera.
- Hiroshi Sugimoto — Theaters, begun 1976. One exposure lasts for an entire film, leaving a luminous screen in which the projected images cease to be individually identifiable.
- Pelle Cass — Selected People, begun 2008. Hundreds of exposures are selectively combined without relocating the depicted figures, producing a still photograph that contains multiple moments in the same space.
- Eadweard Muybridge, Animal Locomotion (1887), used sequential exposures to make bodily motion analyzable.
- Michael Wesely, Open Shutter (2001–2004), used exposures lasting up to several years; transient subjects become nearly invisible while persistent structures remain.

B. Computational photography: the separation is technically unstable

The report says the appropriate technical distinction is not simply brightness versus position. It gives an integral model and a sampled-composite model, then says these describe different operations, not necessarily different locations of delay. It lists: every video frame already integrates light; a long-exposure appearance can be synthesized after capture; multiple sampled poses can be recorded on one plate during exposure; a single sensor image can spatially encode different exposure times (Gu et al. 2010, Coded Rolling Shutter Photography, IEEE ICCP).

It also says an opaque white trajectory is not necessarily the direct result of physical integration. If segmentation, optical flow, masks, or recoloring make the trajectory white, that is a designed representation and must be disclosed.

C. Contemporary photographic theory

- David Campany (2008), Photography and Cinema. Reaktion Books.
- Daniel Rubinstein and Katrina Sluis (2008), A Life More Photographic: Mapping the Networked Image. photographies, 1(1), 9–28.
- Joanna Zylinska (2017), Nonhuman Photography. MIT Press.

The report says these are context, not evidence that anyone made this exact pair.

### 2. What has been tried and failed — or abandoned?

The tool distinguishes a documented limitation from a failed or abandoned project.

- Höferlin et al. (2012): tested frame skipping, temporal blending, object trails, and predictive trajectories with 24 participants. The tool says the assumption that these occupy opposite ends of a trade-off was complicated by the results.
- Eschner et al. (2023): limits to the benefits of adding motion trails.
- Rengarajan et al. (2020): temporal order is hard to recover from a blurred photograph alone; their method adds two short exposures.
- Gu et al. (2010): readout schemes shown in simulation; a programmable sensor was still in development. The tool marks as UNVERIFIED whether the project was later abandoned.

The tool says it found no reliable source that artists or researchers abandoned placing an integral and a sampled representation of one interval side by side. It says digital photomontage, stroboscopic compositing, synthetic long exposure, and action-shot summaries already exist as methods.

### 3. The five works the tool ranks closest

Ranked by the tool as threats, not by the student:

1. Agarwala et al. (2004), Interactive Digital Photomontage. SIGGRAPH / TOG. Combines parts of photographs, including stroboscopic moving objects and time-lapse mosaics. The tool says it is not an argument about perceptual loss between two fixed prints of one interval.
2. Tabellion et al. (2023), Computational Long Exposure Mobile Photography. SIGGRAPH / TOG. A burst becomes a computational long exposure; the system can also output a conventional photograph from the same camera operation. The tool says the conventional photograph is not a Δt sequence of bodies.
3. Höferlin et al. (2012), Evaluation of Fast-Forward Video Visualization. IEEE TVCG. Compares frame skipping, temporal blending, object trails, and predictive trajectories. Video playback, not a pair of prints.
4. Pelle Cass, Selected People (2008 onward). Figures from different moments in one scene. The tool says it does not use a uniform Δt and does not establish a matched continuous-trace counterpart.
5. Étienne-Jules Marey, chronophotography (1880s–1890s). Multiple phases on one plate; sampling can happen during exposure; dark clothing can become white markings.

Sugimoto is discussed and left out of this five. The tool says he shows a duration becoming a visually emptied image, and Cass shows many discrete bodies in one image.

### 4. The tool's gap — not the student's

The tool's claim table, in brief: a photograph having no place for time is not literally defensible; white traces are not novel and not automatic; sampling preserving bodies but losing intervals is only partly defensible; the complementary-loss description may fit these two outputs but is not a general law; "where delay sits determines the type" is technically contradicted as a binary; the two prints as a designed comparison is potentially defensible, and the novelty of this exact pairing is marked UNVERIFIED.

The tool splits "delay" into three quantities: exposure duration τ, sampling interval Δt, and processing latency δ. It says the photographs manipulate τ and Δt, or simulate them, and do not demonstrate that δ determines the visual form.

The tool's one-sentence gap, provisional and not adopted:

> There is no established gap in producing integrated motion traces or sampled body composites; a narrower question remains whether two controlled, physically printed translations of the same recorded interval produce systematically different perceptions of bodily presence, continuity, direction, and loss—and whether that difference can be attributed to specified imaging operations rather than to the artists' visual choices.

The tool suggests a four-condition matrix (faithful blur, white trajectory, repeated bodies, abstract marks) and an observer study. Empirical work of that kind needs ethics approval and is not assumed here.

Reading order suggested by the tool: Agarwala (2004), Höferlin (2012), Tabellion (2023), Freeman and Zhang (2003), Rengarajan (2020), then Cass and Marey.
