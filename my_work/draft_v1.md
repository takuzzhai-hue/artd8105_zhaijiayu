# Re-Developing Memory: The Boundaries of Photographic Records and the Labeling of Generative Content in RAW-Based Photographic Reworking

## Abstract

Photographs organize information about a scene through exposure, optical imaging, and computational processing, whereas personal recollections are shaped by attention, emotion, and time. The divergence between the two provides a creative point of departure for reconsidering the relationship between photographic records and subjective experience. This study proposes a method of photographic reworking driven by personal narratives: recollections are translated into traceable image-processing decisions, with RAW re-development prioritized as a response to the narrative. Where non-generative image operations are needed to express particular content, these operations are identified according to what they actually do. Where the existing record cannot support the desired content, explicitly labeled generative additions are used, or the gaps are left unfilled. The artwork organizes original narrative statements, the artist's interpretations, processing operations, and affected regions into interconnected images and annotations, making the relationships among recording, authorial interpretation, and generated content part of the viewing experience. I developed an image-generation system that uses recollections of the original circumstances to re-develop the image from the RAW stage and rework the photograph. The individual stages of this process are then brought together to produce a photographic presentation of the act of remembering.

Drawing on the format of test prints made during photographic development, I ultimately create a juxtaposition of recollections. The final work takes the form of a photographic artwork composed of intermediate stages of an image being developed repeatedly through memory.

## 1. Introduction: The Divergence Between Photographs and Recollections

At the instant of pressing the shutter, I am not merely looking at a subject about to be recorded; my entire body occupies a space. The temperature that day, the way light moves through the scene, and the memory of having taken a similar photograph in the same place all overlap in my perception of that moment. What remains with me afterward is everything that moment has brought back to mind. The camera, however, records only the light falling within the frame during a single exposure and processes it into an image according to a predetermined program. For Bergson (1991 [1896]), the past has already entered present perception before the shutter is pressed. This gap forms the initial motivation for the study: I want to preserve something of embodied sensation within the machine's image-processing procedures so that the resulting photograph comes closer to the overlapping bodily sensations of memory.

This experience points to a more general problem. The difference between photographs and recollections concerns not only what an image presents, but also what is reorganized in subsequent acts of remembering.

Both sides of this divergence involve selection. On one side is the camera: the signal recorded by the sensor passes through stages including white balance, demosaicing, color transformation, denoising, and tone mapping before becoming a viewable image. These decisions are typically made automatically inside the camera or software, leaving the viewer with only the result. On the other side is recollection: emotion or attention brings certain details to the foreground while others are omitted, and recollections can change through repeated narration and re-viewing. Consequently, a mismatch between a photograph and a recollection does not necessarily mean that the photograph recorded the scene incorrectly or that the memory needs to be corrected. This study treats the discrepancy as a creative prompt rather than an error to be eliminated. “I remember the light being warmer” may guide color adjustment, while “I only remember her face” may guide a redistribution of visual attention within the image. These correspondences are creative interpretations of narrative statements; they cannot retrospectively prove that the camera's original white balance or focus was wrong.

Generative imaging technologies make it easy to supplement and rewrite photographs, but a seamless final image cannot reveal which elements derive from the original record, which result from processing decisions, and which were generated by a model. The study therefore asks: **How can personal narratives intervene in RAW re-development, and how can the artwork make visible the relationships among record-derived processing, authorial interpretation, and generative additions?** The proposed response is a narrative-driven method of photographic reworking: recollections are addressed primarily through RAW reprocessing; where the record cannot support the desired content, generative additions are explicitly labeled or gaps are preserved; and original narrative statements, processing operations, and image regions are linked so that the making process becomes a legible part of the artwork. The contribution is not to verify or reconstruct memory, but to present the negotiation between photographic records and personal recollections as a traceable artistic form. This is the photograph uploaded by the user, together with the prompt, “I feel that this photograph should have stronger contrast and look more vintage. The pendulum should move a little, suggesting the passage of time,” and the generated Figures 1, 2, and 3.

## 2. Theoretical and Artistic Context

This study approaches photography through two dimensions: the photographic apparatus and digital imaging. Flusser (2000 [1983]) describes the camera as an apparatus that executes a “program.” The photographer operates within the possibilities predefined by that program, and images result from the realization of those possibilities. Flusser also suggests that photographic freedom lies in working against the apparatus's program, deliberately producing outcomes the program did not anticipate. Research on imaging pipelines makes this “program” concrete. Karaimer and Brown (2016) observe that internal camera-processing stages are often embedded in hardware, rely on proprietary manufacturer algorithms, and are difficult to access. They therefore developed a software platform that allows RAW images to pass step by step through black-level correction, white balance, demosaicing, color-space conversion, denoising, and tone mapping, while enabling intermediate images and parameters to be inspected and modified at each stage. Together, these perspectives indicate that “development” in digital photography involves choosing among multiple possible renderings rather than revealing an already complete image. This is precisely why this paper uses the term **“re-development”**: RAW data are not yet a viewable image; they become a photograph only through a series of rendering decisions, and those decisions can be made again. Yet re-development has limits. Exposure time, aperture, and the actual plane of focus have already determined what information reaches the sensor. Subsequent adjustments can work only within the constraints of the existing record and available processing. This study therefore limits its interventions to accessible development and image-processing stages and explicitly distinguishes reprocessing from content generation.

Recollection follows a different organizational logic. In “Photography,” Kracauer (1995 [1927]) distinguishes photographs from “memory images”: photography grasps what is given as a spatial (or temporal) continuum, whereas memory images retain what is given only insofar as it has significance. Memory images and photographic representations are therefore in conflict. This distinction corresponds directly to a statement such as “I only remember her face”: what recollection preserves is what matters to the person remembering, not the entire field of vision. Bartlett's (1932) experiments likewise show that remembering is not the reactivation of a fixed trace but a form of “imaginative reconstruction,” shaped by the rememberer's existing schemas and attitudes (p. 213). Kuhn's (1995) concept of “memory work” further treats family photographs as sites where recollections are produced rather than as evidence of memory: looking at photographs itself contributes to the construction of narratives about the past. These arguments support a premise of the present method: statements such as “brighter,” “colder,” or “I only remember one person” may refer to visual attributes, but they may also express feelings that cannot be translated directly into image parameters. Narrative statements cannot be treated as determinate technical instructions. A single statement may correspond to several processing approaches, and the artist must interpret the available possibilities and document how that interpretation affects the resulting image.

The influence between photographs and recollections also works in both directions. Wade et al. (2002) showed participants manipulated childhood photographs in which they had been composited into a hot-air balloon ride that had never taken place. Approximately half subsequently reported full or partial memories of the event in interviews. Pataranutaporn et al. (2025) revisited this issue in the context of generative AI. Two hundred participants first viewed 24 original images and, after a distraction task, were assigned to four groups that viewed either the original images again, AI-edited images, videos generated from the original images, or videos generated from AI-edited images. All three AI conditions produced more false memories than the control condition, with the strongest effect in the edited-image-to-video condition; participants' confidence in inaccurate memories also increased. These findings suggest that modifying one's own photographs in this study might likewise reshape subsequent recollections. They do not, however, establish that labeling generated content would prevent such effects.

Art and design practices have already addressed relationships between generated images and memory. Zhang, Cheng, and Luo's *ReCollection* (first exhibited in 2023, with an associated paper published in SIGGRAPH Art Papers) grew out of an author's experience of witnessing their grandmother's memory decline due to dementia. Participants whisper a fading memory of approximately seven seconds into a microphone. Through speech recognition, automatic text completion, and text-to-image generation, the system assembles an evolving “collective visual memory” in real time, blurring the boundary between “remembering” and “imagining.” Domestic Data Streamers' *Synthetic Memories* project, underway since 2023, uses interviews and generative AI to help people living with dementia, migrants, and others reconstruct visual memories of scenes never photographed or of images that have been lost. Interviewers and narrators iteratively revise the images together. In 2024, the project operated as the “Citizen Office of Synthetic Memories” at the Barcelona Design Museum; it is currently exhibited in MoMA's *Full Disclosure* exhibition in New York. Both projects begin with verbal narratives and generate whole images; an original visual record either does not exist or does not participate in the generation. A CHI study by Wu et al. (2026), by contrast, focuses on existing photographs. Twelve participants used generative AI to edit their own photographs and then took part in semi-structured interviews. The study found that participants valued “felt memories” more than factual accuracy and were willing to modify environments but were reluctant to accept edits that changed people's identities. The editing process itself also became an experience of remembering. The authors further note that, when photographs circulate outside their original contexts, editing intentions and histories may not travel with them. This observation directly informs the present study: its annotations are intended to keep editing histories attached to the image. Unlike these projects, *Chronoscope*, designed by Chen, Odom, and colleagues (Chen et al., 2019; 2023), is a near-eye photographic viewer that uses photographs' temporal metadata. Three dials control viewing direction, time-range mode, and viewing speed, allowing users to encounter photographic archives nonlinearly. It does not alter any pixels; instead, it changes the temporal structure of viewing. It thus provides a point of comparison: the present study intervenes in image development itself while also using annotations to change how the image is viewed.

In contrast to these works, this study takes the RAW data of an existing photograph as both its creative material and its constraint, rather than generating an entire image directly from language. It translates personal narratives into traceable processing choices and incorporates insufficient records, generative additions, and unresolved absences into the final work. The artwork presents not only the modified image but also why it was modified, what materials those modifications relied upon, and which parts involve speculation.

## 3. Artistic Position and Method

This study uses personal photographs and associated recollections as its materials, prioritizing cases in which RAW files are available, recollections of the photographed scene remain relatively clear, and discussable discrepancies exist between the photograph and the recollection. The study includes a generative system for participants to use; participants are recruited to experience it at the exhibition venue. Before a photograph is uploaded, the system explains the intended use of the image and the AI-processing procedures, and processing begins only after the participant has consented. Narratives are audio-recorded and transcribed, then checked by the participants. Participants must approve the final images and annotations before display and may withdraw their authorization at any time.

Recollections are recorded in two steps. First, without looking at the photograph, participants describe their memories of the photographed scene: the clearest details, lighting, colors, atmosphere, objects of attention, and whether they remember anything absent from the photograph. Second, after looking at the photograph, they describe differences between the image and their recollection. The two narratives are stored separately, and the original wording forms the basis for subsequent modifications. Because photographs themselves influence memory (Wade et al., 2002), this procedure can distinguish only descriptions given before and after viewing during the current session; it cannot exclude the effects of earlier encounters with the photograph.

Narratives are grouped into three categories: explicit visual requests, ambiguous expressions, and feelings that cannot be visualized directly. Candidate operations are then developed accordingly. For example, “the lighting was warmer” may correspond to white balance and tonal adjustment; “I only remember her face” may be expressed through local brightness, sharpness, or background blur; and “there was also a bouquet on the table” first requires checking whether the existing record contains relevant information. These mappings are the artist's interpretations, not unique or necessary correspondences. Cross-modal mappings, such as translating “noisy” into image noise, must be identified as metaphorical. If a large language model (LLM) assists with interpretation, each extracted element must be linked to the original narrative wording, and proposed operations must be reviewed by the artist. The model cannot independently establish whether the record contains enough information to support a given modification.

Where the existing record can support a narrative request, processing first proceeds through changes to development parameters, including gain, white balance, color, tone curves, and local luminance. Another group of operations, drawing on Karaimer and Brown's (2016) categorization of pipeline stages, selectively bypasses or reconfigures accessible stages. In Flusser's terms, this means intentionally producing outcomes not anticipated by the program from within the apparatus's program. The purpose is to turn choices within imaging into expressive material, not to claim to restore memory's original visual state. Synthetic blur, downsampling, and black-level lifting can express focused attention, indistinct detail, or a sense of haze, but each operation must be identified according to its actual effect.

When a narrative involves content that the existing record cannot reliably support, the source of that insufficiency must be specified. Adding a person outside the frame or behind an occlusion, reconstructing missing facial detail in an out-of-focus region, or filling in an unseen background after removing an object all involve speculative construction of content. Judgments are made for specific regions and requests rather than classifying an entire photograph as either “inside” or “outside” the record. Where the status cannot be determined, uncertainty is retained rather than automatically resorting to generation. Three responses are available: generative addition, preservation of the gap, or postponement of processing. If generative AI is used, a mask of the generated region is retained, and the result is explicitly labeled as an addition rather than as recovered historical evidence. The saturation ratios, noise ratios, sharpness ratios, and detection-confidence thresholds proposed in the original concept are not currently sufficient to establish a general boundary.

All modifications are documented through the correspondence “original narrative statement — artist's interpretation — processing operation — affected region,” distinguishing RAW-derived processing, other non-generative image operations, and generative additions, while preserving the modification process and successive versions. Wu et al. (2026) note that editing histories often do not travel with photographs; this documentation is intended to keep that history within the artwork. The final images are produced as annotated prints. The annotations both document the making process and contribute to the expression of the relationship between recording and recollection. The proposed format is A2 inkjet printing on matte cotton fine-art paper. Annotations are printed on translucent sheets layered over the images, which viewers can lift. The original photograph is placed nearby at a smaller scale. The intended viewing sequence is to read the original narrative statement, examine the processed image, and finally compare it with the original photograph. After modification, observations are recorded about which elements feel closer to the recollection, which produce a sense of estrangement, and whether the process prompts a new understanding of the original photograph. ## 5. Discussion and Limitations

This study seeks to bring development decisions and boundaries of generation, which usually remain within the production process, into the final image. Viewers can thus not only look at the reworked photograph but also question the provenance of each change. **[To be completed: a discussion grounded in the completed artwork, addressing how annotations specifically change the viewing relationship; whether they produce interruption, juxtaposition, doubt, or new narratives; and how these effects support the paper's argument.]** Such traceability first makes the artist's interpretive authority visible. Personal feelings are difficult to translate directly into technical parameters, and the same statement may lead to several possible modifications. The artist's understanding determines the final image. Even when an LLM assists with interpretation, interpretation does not disappear; instead, an additional stage of translation is introduced. Traceability therefore requires more than saving parameters: it also entails explaining why a particular visual expression was chosen, which alternatives were rejected, and which correspondences are merely the artist's interpretations.

RAW is both a material and a constraint here. It allows some recorded image information to be reorganized, but it does not contain all information about a scene or automatically restore details already lost at capture. The distinction between RAW reprocessing and generative addition identifies the provenance of production; it does not adjudicate whether a memory is true or false. Where only a JPEG is available, necessary metadata are missing, or a narrative cannot be visualized, the method's applicability is limited. Those limitations should be stated rather than assuming that the same workflow can always be performed. Labeling also has limits: the studies by Pataranutaporn et al. (2025) and Wade et al. (2002) both demonstrate that altered photographs may influence later recollections of events. A generative label discloses where content comes from, but this does not establish that viewers will understand the label or refrain from incorporating the generated content into subsequent memories. **[To be completed: the explanations and feedback procedures actually implemented in the study, and whether changes in recollection were observed. A small number of cases can illuminate only experiences within specific creative contexts. If all cases come from a single artist, the study can provide a detailed account of an individual creative process, but cannot generalize to how others remember or respond to images. ## 6. Conclusion

This study proposes a method of photographic reworking driven by personal narratives that combines RAW re-development with labeled generative additions. It translates narratives into traceable processing choices and makes generation, absence, or uncertainty explicit when the existing record cannot support a request. The work's value lies in making this translation visible: how recollections are narrated, how the artist interprets them, and how the image consequently changes. By connecting the original record, processing operations, and generated regions, the study makes the relationship between photographic recording and subjective recollection into artistic material that can be observed and discussed. **[To be completed: the principal findings derived from the actual artworks and case analyses, addressing the research question posed in the introduction. ## Supplementary Submission Material

### Why It Matters

> As generative editing becomes a default feature of camera and phone software, the line between what a sensor recorded and what a model inferred disappears from the final image. This work treats the camera imaging pipeline as an artistic material: personal recollections are translated into traceable RAW development choices, and only where the recorded data cannot support a request is generative content introduced, masked, and labeled—or the gap is left open. By making the provenance of every change part of the artwork, it offers the SIGGRAPH community a practice-based model for keeping recording, interpretation, and generation distinguishable in computational photography.
